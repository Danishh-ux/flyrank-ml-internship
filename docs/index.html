Prioritizing the Review Queue

A content refresh & CTR-recovery score that tells a reviewer where to look first — validated on clients the model never saw, and honest about where it doesn't work.

Lane  Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring Data  FlyRank internship warehouse, March 2026 Validation  Client-held-out split

Abstract

Content teams can't manually review every page in a warehouse of over 300,000, so this project asks which stale, still-visible pages deserve a reviewer's limited time first. I built a ranking model on five features known at decision time — prior-window impressions, clicks, average position, word count, and content age — trained to predict whether a page's visibility would decline over the following two weeks. Tested on 12 clients held out entirely from training, a gradient-boosted model beat a hand-built CTR-vs-position-tier baseline by 12.5% on Precision@50 (0.72 vs. 0.64), while a plain logistic regression did not beat the baseline at all. Overall AUC stayed weak (0.46–0.58) across every approach, meaning none of these features explain page-level decline broadly — but the boosted model is measurably better at surfacing the highest-value review candidates specifically, which is the decision this project supports. The output is a ranked, reason-coded queue, not a verdict: every score ships with an explanation a reviewer can check.

1 ·  Introduction

The decision this supports

A content team lead has a ranked queue and limited review hours each week. They need to know, out of tens of thousands of pages, which ones to pull to the top: refresh it, redirect it, or leave it alone. Nobody has time to read every page, so the prioritization itself has to be automated — the model's job isn't to explain why a page is declining, it's to make sure the pages worth a human's attention actually reach one.

That framing sets an asymmetric cost. A false positive — a flagged page that didn't really need review — wastes a reviewer's time, but it's recoverable next cycle. A false negative — a genuinely declining, high-traffic page that never surfaces — keeps losing visibility unnoticed, which is a more expensive mistake. That asymmetry is why this project optimizes for Precision@K, how clean the top of the queue is, rather than accuracy across the entire page population.

Why Precision@KAn editor works down a ranked list with a fixed time budget — they never see, and never pay for, the bottom of it.

Two windows, one month

To keep features from ever seeing the future, March splits into two internal halves: a decision-moment window (March 1–15) that supplies every feature, and an outcome window (March 16–31) that supplies the label. After that split and a demand filter, 102,639 content items across 38 clients remained, of which 56,899 declined and 45,740 did not.

What was excluded, and why

  • health_score, priority_score, and action_type — not shipped in this release at all.
  • ga4_sessions and other engagement columns for this month — many clients' GA4 tracking starts after this window, so treating "untracked" as "zero engagement" would misrepresent them. Rows are filtered on tracking availability rather than assuming.
  • trend_direction, trend_pct, and the March 16–31 impressions/clicks themselves — these are the label's own source (see the leakage check below).
  • No client names, domains, URLs, or raw exports appear anywhere in the pipeline — only hashed client_hash_id / content_hash_id identifiers throughout.

3 ·  Methodology

Label, features, baseline, and the leakage trap

Label

is_declining = 1 if a page's outcome-window impressions fell below 80% of its decision-window impressions, else 0. Built directly from observed warehouse numbers, not a proxy.

Features (five, all known before the decision moment)

  • imp_prev15 — impressions, March 1–15
  • clk_prev15 — clicks, March 1–15
  • pos_prev15 — average search position, March 1–15
  • word_count — static content property
  • content_age_days_at_decision — derived from creation date

Baseline: CTR-gap × position tier

A page is worth reviewing first if it has real demand (impressions above a volume floor) and its click-through rate sits below the median CTR of other pages at its own position tier. Two signal checks shaped that design. Raw staleness ran opposite to the intuitive assumption — the stalest bucket (181+ days since update) had the lowest observed decline rate at 47.1%, not the highest — and was dropped from the rule entirely. CTR-vs-position-tier, by contrast, held up cleanly and monotonically:

Validation: split by client, not by row

The train/test split is grouped on client_hash_id, so every page from a given client lands entirely on one side — a stricter test of whether a score generalizes to genuinely new clients, not just new pages from clients already seen in training. That produced 40,616 training rows across 26 clients and 62,023 test rows across 12 held-out clients, with zero client overlap between them.

The leakage trap

Before trusting any of this, I deliberately fed the label's own source column back in as a "feature" on a separate random split, to see what would happen. Honest AUC on the five clean features was 0.639; adding the leakage column pushed AUC to 1.000 — the model wasn't learning a pattern, it was reading back its own answer key. That column, trend_direction, and trend_pct are excluded from every model reported below.

Signal checkStaleness alone pointed the wrong way and was cut. Trust the CTR×tier signal because it survived that check — not because it was the first idea.

The honest read

HistGradientBoosting wins on the metric that matters here: a 12.5% relative lift in Precision@50 over the baseline rule (0.64 → 0.72), and Precision@20 rises from 0.60 to 0.75. Logistic Regression, notably, does not beat the baseline (0.54 vs. 0.64) — a linear model can't capture whatever nonlinear interaction the boosted-tree model is picking up across these five features, and that underperformance is reported rather than hidden.

All three approaches have low AUC (0.46–0.58), close to random. That isn't a contradiction of the Precision@K numbers above: AUC measures ranking quality across the entire test set, while Precision@K measures only the top of the queue — the part a reviewer actually acts on. None of these approaches explain page-level decline broadly, but HistGradientBoosting is measurably better at surfacing the highest-value review candidates specifically, which is the decision this project was built to support.

Reading AUC hereNear-random AUC + strong Precision@50 isn't a contradiction — it means the signal lives at the top of the ranking, not across the whole population.

5 ·  Limitations

What this cannot claim

No causal claim

Nothing here shows that refreshing a page causes recovery, or that any score reverse-engineers a search ranking algorithm. This is observational data; a real experiment would be needed for a causal claim.

Short, single-month window

A 15-vs-15-day split inside one March 2026 partition can mistake a mid-month blip — an update ripple, a seasonal dip, a competitor's temporary push — for real decline. A longer window across multiple months would separate noise from persistent trend.

Unbalanced client panel

Tracking start dates vary by client (GSC starts span 2025‑01‑27 to 2026‑06‑02 across 104 clients). Clients who joined tracking later can look artificially flat here simply because they have less history behind them, not because they're genuinely stable.

A real, unresolved anomaly

9 of the baseline rule's original top‑10 picks had CTR of exactly 0.0, and more broadly 1,157 pages combine real search volume (≥1,000 impressions) with a literal zero click count. The current model can't distinguish a genuinely fixable CTR problem from a possible tracking or attribution issue — that judgment call still needs a human reviewer before acting on the top of the queue.

Overall AUC is weak

0.46–0.58 across every approach tried. These features explain the top of the ranking reasonably (Precision@K), not page-level decline broadly. This is a decision-support ranking for where limited review time is best spent — not a general predictive model of content decline.

Every finding above is observed, directional, and decision-support — a prioritized list to start from, not a verdict on any individual page.

Before actingZero-CTR pages get triaged separately, first — see the playbook in the next section.

Playbook, in order

  1. Start at the top of the HistGradientBoosting-ranked queue, but triage any page with ctr == 0.0 and clicks == 0 separately first — confirm it's a real content problem and not a tracking/attribution gap before spending a title or snippet rewrite on it.
  2. Work down ctr_underperformer_visible pages next — highest demand, confirmed CTR gap.
  3. Move to ctr_underperformer_moderate once the visible tier is cleared.
  4. Treat visible_review_candidate pages as monitor_or_expand — not urgent, but worth a content-expansion look since they already convert at or above their tier's norm.
  5. Leave low_priority_monitor pages alone this cycle; revisit if their demand grows.
Why keep the baseline's codesThe winning model scores pages; it doesn't explain them. Reason codes are what make the queue actionable rather than just ranked.
